## Context

Ocean spaces cover 71% of the Earth’s surface and are central to globalization, connecting societies across the planet. An essential infrastructure of the global economy, they carry nearly 90% of global trade, provide 20% of the protein consumed worldwide, and contribute to more than a quarter of the world’s oil and gas supply.

Yet while these few examples underline how central the oceans are to multiple social, economic and political processes, they long remained peripheral to a geographical science that has historically “written the land” more than the sea, interpreting its etymology very literally. Physical geography and the natural sciences have largely invested marine territories, revealing in particular their fundamental role in regulating global climate balances. But human geography, a discipline otherwise well suited to analysing them, has kept these spaces at a distance, treating them as peripheral objects rather than as legitimate and central fields of enquiry.

Today the oceans are undergoing profound change, largely driven by climate change. For example, the Arctic Ocean is warming on average four times faster than the rest of the globe, summer sea-ice extent follows a sustained downward trend, and global sea-level rise is accelerating, averaging 4.5 mm per year between 2013 and 2021. It is also estimated that, to date, the oceans have absorbed 40% of emissions from fossil-fuel combustion and 90% of the heat from anthropogenic global warming. These major and multifaceted changes are a significant challenge for existing and emerging governance structures, which are at the heart of the research approach.

In his *Géographie de l’océan global*, Vanney introduced an integrated vision of the ocean, stating in the introduction that this notion of globality brings together all aspects of the ocean: components (solid, fluid, living), movement, “events”, and so on, uniting them in a long “feedback causality”, across the various scales of time and space, from the local to the planetary (p. xi; our translation). This volumetric and multiscalar perspective frames this research programme.

## Mission

The mission of this chair is to broaden our understanding of oceanic spatial dynamics by repositioning human and political geography at the heart of maritime studies. It aims to develop innovative theoretical frameworks and suitable investigative methods to grasp the volumetric, mobile and multiscalar complexity of the ocean, drawing on interdisciplinary and international collaborations to shed light on governance issues related to adaptation to climate change, in maritime spaces in particular.

## Vision

To create a university hub in the human and political geography of the oceans, able to offer complementary insight into climate and maritime governance issues, and to contribute to the reach of UQAR’s expertise in knowledge of ocean and coastal spaces.

## Values

Scientific rigour, interdisciplinarity, accessibility of science and equity. Beyond these values expected in research, the approach taken in this chair also adheres, together with the other chairholders of the TAC programme, to principles of sobriety, engagement and responsibility, fully acknowledging that the place of the scientist in society is limited neither to their research nor to academic circles alone.

## Objectives

To develop a human and political geography of ocean spaces that complements UQAR’s excellence in knowledge of maritime spaces.

1. Analyse how governance structures for ocean spaces, whether in place or under negotiation, integrate the specificities of the spatial dynamics of these environments.
2. Consider more effective governance structures to support adaptation to climate change in ocean and coastal spaces.
3. Contribute to training a new generation of researchers and professionals with a fine understanding of the specificity of maritime environments.
